# BREACH Student Language Standard

Use this standard for every mission, including future Mission 5 content, hints, feedback, evidence labels, reports, and teacher-facing student directions.

## Audience and tone

BREACH is designed for high school cybersecurity students, including beginners and dual-credit students. Write in clear, direct language that sounds like a real investigation briefing. Do not write as if every student already knows college-level cybersecurity vocabulary.

## Writing rules

- Use short sentences and familiar words.
- Give one main instruction at a time.
- Tell students what to look at, what to do, and what they should decide.
- Explain a new technical term in plain language the first time it appears.
- Keep the correct cybersecurity term after explaining it. Students should learn the real vocabulary.
- Use examples from the fictional RooBank incident to make abstract ideas easier to understand.
- Keep feedback helpful and specific. Explain why an answer works or does not work.
- Avoid unnecessary formal phrases such as "correlate indicators," "establish provenance," "defensible assessment," "proportionate response," and "prioritize evidence collection." Say what students should actually compare, save, decide, or report.
- Avoid long paragraphs. Break multi-step directions into numbered steps or short sections.
- Make report prompts clear about what information students must include.

## Keep the challenge

Simpler language must not mean easier cybersecurity thinking. Keep the evidence, puzzles, technical tasks, and decision-making meaningful. Difficulty should come from the investigation, not from confusing directions or unexplained vocabulary.

## Example wording

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| Correlate the DNS and endpoint telemetry. | Compare the website lookup records with the computer's activity logs. |
| Establish evidence provenance. | Find out where the file came from and who handled it. |
| Assess the integrity of the artifact. | Check whether the file has changed. |
| Recommend a proportionate containment action. | Choose an action that stops the problem without shutting down more than needed. |
| Distinguish confirmed findings from hypotheses. | Separate what the evidence proves from what you still suspect. |
| Analyze potential lateral movement. | Check whether an attacker tried to move from one computer to another. |

## Technical vocabulary

Keep important terms such as phishing, malware, hash, SHA-256, steganography, encryption, certificate, PKI, DNS, SMB, lateral movement, containment, and recovery. Explain each term in everyday language before expecting students to use it independently.

## Mission continuity

All five missions should use the same general reading level and tone. Rookie, Security Analyst, and Incident Commander paths may differ in the amount of guidance, evidence, and technical depth, but none should rely on unnecessarily complex wording.
